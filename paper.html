Bayesian Hierarchical Inference of AI Exposure Effects on Global Wage Trajectories with Uncertainty
Mejbah Ahammad
Lead AI Instructor and Research Scientist
Independent Research Workspace
GitHub: ahammadmejbah
Abstract— Generative artificial intelligence has renewed questions about how technology changes the distribution of work and wages. This paper documents a reproducible research design for studying associations between occupational AI exposure and reported wage trajectories. The planned analysis would join salary snapshots to a validated occupational exposure index and estimate heterogeneous associations with a Bayesian hierarchical model across countries, sectors, roles, experience levels, and years. As verified on September 9, 2026, the repository contains 71,913 salary records spanning 2020–2025. This release reports descriptive salary summaries and transparent data-quality diagnostics only. The external exposure index, longitudinal person linkage, fitted Bayesian model, posterior samples, and causal identification strategy are not included; therefore, no AI wage effect is claimed.
Index Terms— generative AI, wages, hierarchical Bayesian modeling, measurement error, labor economics, uncertainty quantification, occupational exposure.

I. INTRODUCTION

Generative AI systems are being adopted across occupations that differ in task composition, institutional context, and bargaining power. The same system may automate routine cognitive tasks, complement experienced workers, or create new demand for technical and creative capabilities. A single global AI wage premium therefore risks hiding heterogeneous effects and overstating the precision of available evidence.

This study asks: What is the posterior distribution of exposure effects after accounting for salary uncertainty? How do country, sector, role, and experience moderate the relationship? Does model averaging improve prediction over fixed-effects baselines? Which groups have the greatest posterior probability of wage compression or augmentation?

II. RELATED WORK

Research on technology and labor markets distinguishes task exposure from realized displacement. Occupational exposure indices, firm adoption studies, and wage-distribution analyses each provide useful but incomplete views. Recent work also emphasizes that generative AI can augment some tasks while substituting for others [1], [2].

Two gaps motivate this design. Salary reports are often treated as exact even though they contain measurement and selection error. Sparse country-role cells are often handled with fixed effects that cannot share information efficiently. A hierarchical Bayesian model represents both forms of uncertainty directly [3].

III. RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

A. Research Questions

The analysis estimates global and subgroup exposure associations, evaluates moderation by geography and occupation, compares predictive specifications, and computes posterior probabilities of negative or positive effects.

B. Research Workflow

The workflow validates source records, joins roles to an external exposure score, constructs a panel, fits baseline and hierarchical models, performs posterior checks, and evaluates predictions on held-out time or country-year cells.

IV. DATASET AND EXPERIMENTAL SETUP

A. Dataset Description

As verified on September 9, 2026, the current file contains 71,913 salary records, 104 employee-residence groups, 11 role-family values, and a median USD salary of $138,750. The years represented are 2020 through 2025. Fields include USD salary, job title, role family, employee residence, experience level, work mode, employment type, remote ratio, company location, company size, and ISCO group hint.

B. Data Availability

The source dataset is available at Kaggle: AI Impact on Jobs and Salaries (2020–2026). The implementation and dashboard are available in the author's GitHub repository.

V. DATA PREPROCESSING

Year and USD salary are coerced to numeric values. Records without a usable year or salary are excluded from the analysis frame. Positive salary is transformed as log salary to reduce right skew. Missing role and country labels are retained under explicit categories when possible. Duplicate records, impossible values, and cell coverage must be audited before causal modeling.

A. Measurement Error

Self-reported salaries are treated as noisy observations of latent compensation. A role-specific scale permits reporting variance to differ across occupations; repeated reports or source-quality metadata should inform that scale in a future release.

B. Data Dictionary

The CSV uses work_year for the snapshot year; experience_level and its label for seniority; employment_type and its label for contract type; salary and salary_currency for source compensation; salary_in_usd for the converted analysis value; employee_residence and company_location for geography; remote_ratio and work_mode for work arrangement; company_size for size category; role_family for normalized occupation; and isco_group_hint for the available occupation-code hint. In the current file, 40,540 ISCO hints are missing; all other listed fields have zero missing values after reading the CSV.

VI. EXPLORATORY DATA ANALYSIS

The trajectory and group comparisons are descriptive exhibits. They may reflect changing sample composition, reporting behavior, exchange-rate conversion, or role mix, and should not be interpreted as an AI treatment effect.

VII. FEATURE ENGINEERING

The application computes log salary, categorical group identifiers, and group-level standard errors at runtime. It also computes a transparent title-based exposure screening score for visualization; this score is not a field in Jobs.csv, has not been validated against an occupational classification, and is not an exposure treatment for causal analysis. Titles containing AI, machine learning, software, developer, analytics, research, and related terms receive a higher screening score; broader technology and product terms receive a moderate score; other titles receive a lower score.

The title-based score is a screening feature only. It is not the Gmyrek et al. occupational exposure index and must not be used as a causal treatment variable.

VIII. PROPOSED METHODOLOGY

Let y denote log salary for country i, sector j, role k, year t, and experience level l. The proposed observation model is:

yijktl ~ Normal(muijktl, sigmarole,k)
mu = alpha + beta x + alphacountry,i + alphasector,j + alpharole,k + betacountry,ix + gammayear,t + deltaexperience,l

Group-level priors provide partial pooling. Country slopes capture context-dependent exposure associations, role-level scales represent reporting noise, and autoregressive year effects capture temporal dependence. Shrinkage priors regularize weakly identified exposure coefficients.

IX. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP

Baselines should include pooled regression, country and role fixed effects, regularized linear models, and a tree-based predictive benchmark. Hierarchical models should use four chains, prior predictive checks, posterior predictive checks, R-hat, effective sample size, divergence counts, energy diagnostics, and time-aware held-out validation.

A. Evaluation Metrics

Report held-out log predictive density, RMSE or MAE on log salary, interval coverage, interval width, calibration, and the effective sample size behind each probability statement.

Leave-one-out cross-validation provides predictive scores and stacking weights. Ensemble intervals must include within-model posterior uncertainty and between-model disagreement.

XII. DISCUSSION

Observed differences between role or proxy groups can reflect occupation mix, country composition, seniority, company selection, and reporting behavior. The appropriate interpretation is therefore conditional and probabilistic. A null or uncertain result would be scientifically informative and should not be replaced by a stronger narrative.

XIII. IMPLICATIONS AND APPLICATIONS

A validated model could support aggregate workforce scenario planning, reskilling prioritization, compensation benchmarking, and policy analysis. Outputs should include uncertainty and coverage warnings. They should not be used to score individual workers, automate employment decisions, or label people as replaceable.

XIV. LIMITATIONS

Coverage is uneven across countries, roles, years, and experience levels. Salary reports may reflect selection, nonresponse, and unobserved company characteristics. USD conversion can hide purchasing-power differences. The title proxy can misclassify exposure, and the current file does not provide repeated-person identifiers or a fully defined causal identification strategy.

XV. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

This work establishes a reproducible bridge between labor-market exploration and Bayesian hierarchical inference. Future work will add the validated exposure index, finalize role and ISCO mappings, fit the PyMC or Stan model, compare baselines, validate on held-out years, and publish posterior samples and diagnostics.
